Lightning TDOA Simulator

Simulation of a three-station VLF lightning detection network using TDOA multilateration, and a study of the accuracy achievable under controlled noise.

Context

Originally a hardware project: a three-node VLF lightning detector with loop antennas, ESP32 acquisition boards and GPS modules for time synchronisation. The prototype reached a functional stage but was never finalised, and the original code was not kept.

This simulator reconstructs and formalises the algorithmic part that survived (the geometry, the noise model, the solver and the benchmarks) to answer the questions the prototype never had time to settle empirically.

The principle fits in one sentence: three stations timestamp the impulse, two time differences draw two hyperbolas, and their intersection gives the position. What drew me in is the gap between this idea, simple to state, and the precision its use demands. Everything comes down to synchronisation and timestamping.

Approach

A lightning strike radiates a VLF impulse that travels at ~c; three synchronised receivers see it arrive at slightly different instants. For a source at p and a station at rᵢ:

t_i = || p − r_i || / c  +  n_i

The solver works on the differences in time relative to a reference station, which removes the absolute origin. Two solvers are implemented behind the same API: a non-linear least-squares minimiser (SciPy, the production path, scalable to N stations) and a closed-form intersection of the two hyperbolas (reference and control).

The simulation deliberately stops at arrival times — no waveform synthesis. The goal is to expose the geometric structure of TDOA. The simplifying assumptions (straight-line propagation at constant c, 2D geometry, independent Gaussian noise) are stated explicitly, because they condition how the numbers should be read.

A design point that matters: solver.py does not import simulator.py. The solver consumes a simple dict[station_id, t_arrival]. Wiring up real receivers later = writing a new producer, without touching the solver.

Result

  • Three stations on a 50 km equilateral baseline, 100 ns noise: median error ~52 m inside the triangle, < 65 m out to the circumscribed circle.
  • An essentially 1 log-log slope over four decades: the linear scaling expected at fixed geometry.
  • Optimal network size: side ≈ 5× the radius of the area to cover — stations must be far apart, not clustered.
  • The two solvers agree to within 5%; NLLS is kept because it generalises to N stations.

Demo & code

The web demo is a static site (HTML/CSS/JS + Pyodide): the Python library is loaded as-is in the browser, with no JavaScript port. You move the three stations, trigger storms, and watch the solver work.
